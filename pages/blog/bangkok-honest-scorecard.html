Deep Dive #1: Food — The Perfect 10

Photo: Unsplash

Bangkok gets a perfect 10 on food and there is genuinely nothing to argue about. The city does street food at a level that no other city in the world matches — not in variety, not in price, not in consistency of quality. A plate of pad see ew from a wok on Silom Road costs ₹80–120. A bowl of tom kha at a shophouse in the old city costs ₹100. A mango sticky rice from a cart near the weekend market costs ₹60. All of these will be among the best things you eat all year.

The variety is the other thing. Bangkok is not just Thai food. The Chinatown (Yaowarat) strip alone covers dim sum, roasted duck, braised pork leg, and oyster omelettes. The area around Sukhumvit has Japanese ramen at ₹400, Korean BBQ at ₹700, and Middle Eastern shawarma at ₹250. Indian travellers specifically: the food is vegetarian-friendly to a degree that surprises most people. Pad thai, mango salads, green papaya salads, spring rolls — ask for no fish sauce and most stalls accommodate without drama. This is why Thailand as a whole consistently tops Indian traveller surveys — the food does most of the work.

The rule: Eat where the plastic chairs are. If there are cloth napkins and a QR code menu, you are paying 4x for atmosphere. The food is the same, sometimes worse.

Deep Dive #2: Getting Around — Good, With One Catch

Photo: Unsplash

Bangkok's BTS Skytrain and MRT metro are genuinely excellent — clean, air-conditioned, punctual, and cheap (₹25–80 per ride). If your hotel is on the BTS or MRT line, you can move across the city faster than in most Indian metros. The problem is that Bangkok's tourist sights are not all on the metro. The Grand Palace, Wat Pho, Wat Arun, Chatuchak market, the canal district — these are off the main BTS/MRT grid, which means you will be in a Grab or a tuk-tuk for at least part of every day.

Grab works well and the prices are honest. A 20-minute Grab ride across Sukhumvit runs ₹180–280 without surge, ₹400–600 during peak evening hours. Tuk-tuks are fun exactly once and should be negotiated firmly before you get in — a fair price is 50–80 THB (₹115–185) for a short hop; if the driver quotes 200 THB and mentions a "special shop" on the way, step out. The real frustration is Bangkok's traffic, which is not the BTS's fault but will eat your afternoon if you rely on road transport between 5pm and 8pm. Plan culture-heavy activities in the morning, food and markets in the evening. Malaysia's Grab situation is similar — the pattern of tourist areas being off the main transit grid is consistent across Southeast Asia.

Deep Dive #3: Culture & Sights — Genuinely Deep, Genuinely Crowded

Photo: Unsplash

Bangkok's cultural depth is real and it scores 9/10 only because the crowds at the top three temples have become genuinely difficult. The Grand Palace complex, Wat Pho, and Wat Arun are extraordinary — the architecture, the scale, the ornamentation — there is nothing quite like them in the world. But the Grand Palace now sees 30,000–40,000 visitors a day. The photo you want — the Emerald Buddha Hall against a clear sky, no one in frame — requires you to be through the gates by 8:15am at the latest. By 9:30 there are selfie sticks every four metres and tour group audio guides competing at volume.

The way around this is to treat Bangkok's culture like layers. Layer one is the famous temples — go early, spend 2 hours, leave before the crowd peaks. Layer two is the canal districts and old city neighbourhoods — Thonburi side, the flower market, the amulet market near Wat Mahathat — almost no tourists, genuinely atmospheric, and better for understanding how the city actually works. Layer three is Bangkok's modern cultural scene — the MOCA, the Jim Thompson House, the Bangkokian Museum — all excellent and rarely crowded. Three full days gives you all three layers. Most tourists never leave layer one. Plan your Bangkok itinerary accordingly.

Final Verdict

Bangkok scores 8.1 / 10.
Go. Without hesitation.

It is one of the best-value major cities on earth, with world-class food, a functioning transit system, and enough cultural and culinary depth to reward 5–7 days easily. The crowds at tourist sights and the heat from March to May are the only real friction points. Go November to February. Stay on a BTS line. Eat where the plastic chairs are. It will deliver.

6 things to do differently
TIP 01

Book a BTS-line hotel. Asok, Phrom Phong, or Silom. Everything else is either overpriced or inaccessible.

TIP 02

Grand Palace at 8am. The gates open at 8:30. Queue at 8:15. By 10am it is impractical.

TIP 03

Fill the TDAC before you fly. Thailand Digital Arrival Card online, 72 hours before departure. Not optional.

TIP 04

Eat on the street. ₹80–150 per dish, better than most restaurants. Avoid anywhere with photos on the menu near temples.

TIP 05

Don't plan evenings on Grab. Bangkok traffic 5–8pm is a parking lot. Walk, BTS, or eat near where you already are.

TIP 06

Go November–February. 28–32°C, dry, manageable. March–May is 38°C+ and genuinely unpleasant to sightsee in.

Common questions

Yes — Bangkok scores 8.1/10 overall. It is one of the best-value major cities in Asia, with exceptional food, a functional metro, and genuine cultural depth. The main drawbacks are the heat, the crowds in tourist areas, and some overpriced traps near the big temples. Go with a plan and it delivers.

3 full days covers the essential temples, markets, and neighbourhoods. 4–5 days lets you go deeper — day trips to Ayutthaya, Chatuchak market, the canal districts. Most people underestimate the city's size and overpack their itinerary.

November to February is the best window — dry, cooler (28–32°C), manageable humidity. March and April are brutally hot (38°C+). May to October is rainy season — heavy afternoon showers, but mornings are often clear and hotels are cheapest.

Generally yes. Bangkok scores 7/10 on safety. Petty theft near tourist areas (tuk-tuk scams, gem scams, the 'Grand Palace is closed today' hustle) are the main risks — not violent crime. The metro and main areas are safe at night. Lone women travellers report feeling safe with standard precautions.
