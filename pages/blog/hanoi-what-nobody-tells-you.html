Hanoi: What Nobody Tells You Before You Go

September 2026 · 11 min read · 12 things to know
Share WhatsApp X

Every Hanoi guide tells you to visit Hoan Kiem Lake and eat pho. Here's what they leave out — the traffic logic that will save your life, the ₹30 beer corner that locals love, the dish Obama actually ordered, and why every Halong Bay booking guide on the internet is giving you bad advice.

01 / 12

The Old Quarter's 36 Streets Are Named After What Was Sold There — And It Still Works

Hanoi's Old Quarter isn't just a tourist zone with a catchy nickname. It was literally organised by trade for over 1,000 years — Hang Bac (silver street), Hang Dao (silk street), Hang Thiec (tin street), Hang Ma (paper offerings street). The logic still holds today: paper goods on Hang Ma, silk and fabric on Hang Gai, jewellery on Hang Bac.

Wander with that in mind and the neighbourhood transforms from "busy old streets" into a living museum of trade. Find what street sells what you want, and you'll get a better price than the first shop you stumble into — because you'll know there are 15 more behind it.

Insider tip: Hang Ma street comes alive before Tet (Vietnamese Lunar New Year) and mid-autumn festival with paper lanterns, decorations and offerings. If your trip coincides with either, it's one of the most visually spectacular streets in Asia.
02 / 12

Crossing the Road Is a Philosophy, Not a Skill

Hanoi's traffic terrifies every first-time visitor and becomes completely intuitive by day three. The key insight: motorbikes are fluid, not aggressive. They will flow around you if you move predictably. The worst thing you can do is stop suddenly or make erratic movements.

The technique: make eye contact with oncoming traffic (they need to see you to predict you), step into the road at a slow and steady pace, never stop or change speed suddenly, and let the bikes read your trajectory. Don't wait for a gap — a gap large enough for a pedestrian in Indian traffic standards will never come. Step in when it's manageable and trust the flow.

Where Indians have an edge: If you've survived crossing roads in Delhi, Mumbai or Kolkata, Hanoi traffic will feel structured by comparison. The difference is that Vietnamese drivers are genuinely paying attention — they're not honking at you, they're communicating with you.
03 / 12

Bia Hoi Corner Is One of the Great Cheap Experiences on Earth

At the intersection of Ta Hien and Luong Ngoc Quyen streets, you'll find what locals call Bia Hoi Corner — the intersection of a dozen street-side beer stalls serving fresh-brewed bia hoi (local draught beer) on tiny plastic stools, for ₹30-50 a glass. Fresh-brewed means brewed that morning, low alcohol (~3%), and completely unpretentious.

It's not a tourist trap masquerading as local — it genuinely is where locals and travellers mix in the most natural way. Come at 6pm, squeeze onto a small stool at the edge of the road, order bia hoi and whatever snacks the stall is frying, and watch the neighbourhood evening unfold around you. Stay for two hours. It'll cost you ₹200.

No other city in the world offers this at this price. This alone is a reason to go to Hanoi.

04 / 12

Bun Cha Is Better Than Pho — And Locals Know It

Every Hanoi guide leads with pho. Pho is excellent. But bun cha is Hanoi's signature dish, and it's the one that will actually make you understand why people go to Vietnam specifically to eat. Grilled pork patties and fatty pork slices, served in a light sweet-savoury broth alongside cold rice noodles and a plate of fresh herbs. You mix it yourself at the table.

This is the dish that Anthony Bourdain ordered when he sat across from Barack Obama at a tiny Hanoi restaurant in 2016. That restaurant — Bun Cha Huong Lien, on Le Van Huu street — is still there, still serves the same dish, still costs about ₹150. It's slightly touristy now, but the food hasn't changed.

Vegetarian note: Bun cha is pork-based — not adaptable. For a veg equivalent, look for bun cha chay at Buddhist vegetarian restaurants, or try bun rieu (crab and tomato noodle soup) which has a lighter taste profile.
05 / 12

Go to Hoan Kiem Lake at 6am, Not 10am

Hoan Kiem Lake is Hanoi's most photographed spot, and at 10am in peak season it's wall-to-wall tourists. At 6am, it's something else entirely. Local women doing tai chi in groups. Couples walking the lakeside path. Old men playing chess. Street vendors setting up. The Ngoc Son Temple catching the morning light with almost no one around it.

The lake is beautiful. The lake at sunrise, before the tour groups arrive, is one of the best free experiences in Southeast Asia. Set an alarm. You won't regret it.

On weekend evenings (Friday–Sunday), the streets around Hoan Kiem are closed to traffic and become a pedestrian zone with food stalls, live music, and a genuine street festival atmosphere. Come back then too.

06 / 12

Hanoi Weather Is Genuinely Unpredictable — Pack Accordingly

Unlike the tropical-hot predictability of Bangkok or Bali, Hanoi has four distinct seasons. October–December: cool, dry, pleasant (best time). January–March: the "drizzle season" — grey, damp, chilly (bring layers). April–May: warming up, some rain. June–August: hot, humid, heavy rain.

The surprise for most Indian travellers is cold. December and January mornings in Hanoi can drop to 10–13°C. If you're from Mumbai or Chennai, that's genuinely cold. North India travellers will be fine, but bring a jacket regardless — air conditioning in restaurants and hotels in Hanoi is set for Vietnamese winter logic, which is strange.

Best window from India: October to December — dry, cool, and the most comfortable for walking the Old Quarter all day. March–April is the second-best window before the heat builds.
07 / 12

Egg Coffee Is a Hanoi Original — Nothing Like You Expect

Ca phe trung (egg coffee) was invented in Hanoi in 1946 when milk was scarce and a bartender at the Sofitel Metropole started whipping egg yolks with sugar and coffee. The result is a thick, sweet, custard-like foam served over strong Vietnamese coffee. It sounds odd. It tastes like a warm tiramisu in a cup.

You drink it slowly, with a small spoon, from a tiny glass. The tourist version at places like Giang Café (the original, on a hidden alley off Nguyen Huu Huan street) costs ₹80-120. Worth every rupee. Order the hot version in winter, iced in summer.

It's become popular enough that you'll find it in HCMC and Hoi An now, but Hanoi is where it was born and where it's done best. Don't leave without trying it.

08 / 12

Book Halong Bay In Person in Hanoi — Not Online

This will save you ₹3,000-8,000 per person. The same 2-night cruise that costs ₹15,000+ on booking platforms costs ₹8,000-12,000 when you walk into a tour operator in the Old Quarter and negotiate in person. The cruise companies are the same — you're just cutting out the online platform markup.

Walk down Ma May or Hang Be street, get quotes from 4-5 operators for the same class of cruise (budget/mid-range/luxury), check their TripAdvisor reviews on your phone while they talk to you, and book with the one who shows you the boat on a brochure and gives you the best rate. Same boats, same experience, significantly lower price.

Crowd hack: Ask operators specifically about Lan Ha Bay or Bai Tu Long Bay instead of the main Halong Bay area. Fewer boats, same limestone karst scenery, and significantly less crowded. Some operators will try to steer you to the main bay — push back.
09 / 12

Hanoi Is Not a Party City — And That's Fine

If you're expecting Bangkok-style nightlife, Hanoi will disappoint you. The city generally winds down by midnight — even Ta Hien street (the backpacker bar area) is quiet by 1am. The drinking culture is bia hoi and early evenings, not clubs and bottle service.

What Hanoi does offer instead: one of the most liveable, walkable, atmospheric evening experiences in Southeast Asia. The French Quarter at night is beautiful. The lake is peaceful. The Old Quarter's maze of lanes is endlessly explorable. It's a city for wandering, eating, and drinking slowly — not for dancing until 4am.

If nightlife is a priority, plan for Ho Chi Minh City at the end of your Vietnam trip. Bui Vien Street in HCMC is everything Hanoi is not on that front. Check our full breakdown in the Vietnam Honest Scorecard.

10 / 12

The Train Street Is Real — But Go Early and With Low Expectations

Hanoi's famous Train Street — a narrow residential alley where a live train passes inches from the houses and café tables — went through a period of being closed to tourists (2019–2022) after Instagram made it dangerously overcrowded. It's partially reopened, but with restrictions. The cafés that line the track charge an entry fee (₹50-100) to sit and wait for the train.

The train passes twice a day in each direction. Go in the morning when the crowd is smaller. The experience itself is genuinely surreal — a full-sized train at walking speed through a lane where you could touch both walls with your arms spread. Worth seeing once, even if the Instagram version is more dramatic than reality.

11 / 12

Hanoi Is Cheaper Than Ho Chi Minh City — Use That

Most travellers assume all of Vietnam is equally priced. It isn't. Hanoi is meaningfully cheaper than HCMC on accommodation (10-20% lower for equivalent quality), street food (slightly cheaper), and tours. If budget is a real consideration, spend more nights in the north.

A comfortable mid-range hotel in the Old Quarter runs ₹2,000-3,500/night. Street food breakfast (pho or banh mi) costs ₹50-100. Grab across the city costs ₹80-200 per ride. You can live very well in Hanoi on ₹2,500-3,000/day including accommodation.

12 / 12

Two Days Is Never Enough — Plan for Three

Every itinerary for Vietnam allocates 2 days to Hanoi. It works, but you'll leave feeling like you skimmed it. Three days lets you do the Old Quarter properly (day 1), a day trip to the Perfume Pagoda or Ninh Binh (day 2), and a slow morning at the lake, a museum, and the French Quarter (day 3) before your Halong Bay cruise or onward flight.

The travellers who say Hanoi is overrated are almost always the ones who gave it 48 hours. The city reveals itself slowly — through meals, through wandering, through the rhythm of a neighbourhood that doesn't perform for tourists.

Give it time. Compared to any other destination in this region — Japan, Bali, Bangkok — Hanoi offers the most per rupee of genuine, unhurried cultural experience. See how it stacks up in our Vietnam Honest Scorecard.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Hanoi safe for tourists? +
Yes, Hanoi is generally very safe. The main risks are petty theft in crowded areas and motorbike bag snatching. Traffic is the biggest practical danger — but Vietnamese drivers are predictable once you understand the flow. Violent crime against tourists is very rare.
How many days do you need in Hanoi? +
Minimum 2 days for the city itself, plus 2 nights for a Halong Bay cruise if you plan to do it. Three days in Hanoi is much more comfortable — it lets you explore the Old Quarter, do a day trip to Ninh Binh, and visit the French Quarter without rushing.
What is Hanoi famous for food? +
Bun cha (grilled pork and noodles in broth), pho Hanoi (cleaner and less sweet than southern pho), banh mi, cha ca (turmeric fish with dill), and egg coffee (ca phe trung) — a Hanoi original invented in 1946. Don't leave without trying all five.
What is the best area to stay in Hanoi? +
The Old Quarter (Hoan Kiem district) is best for first-timers — walkable to most sights, restaurants, and tour offices. It's noisy and busy. For a quieter base, try the French Quarter (Ba Dinh) or Tay Ho (West Lake) area, which has better boutique hotels and a more local feel.
Should I book Halong Bay from Hanoi or online? +
Book in person from operators in Hanoi's Old Quarter — you'll save ₹3,000-8,000 per person vs online platforms. Walk Ma May or Hang Be street, get 4-5 quotes, check TripAdvisor reviews on your phone, and negotiate. Same cruises, same boats, significantly lower price.
