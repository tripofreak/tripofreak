Japan broke me in the best possible way. I arrived expecting temples and bullet trains. I didn't expect to cry at a bowl of ramen, feel genuinely safe walking alone at 2am in Tokyo, or find myself bowing involuntarily by day three. Japan doesn't just meet expectations — it quietly recalibrates them.

But it's not perfect. The crowds at Kyoto in peak season are a special kind of chaos. Finding vegetarian food is harder than you'd think. And until recently, the price tag kept many Indian travellers away. That's changed. The yen has dropped ~30% against the rupee since 2022. Japan in 2026 is the most accessible it's been in a decade. Here's the full, unfiltered scorecard.

Culture 10 / 10

Japan earns a perfect 10 here without debate. This is a civilisation so distinct, so meticulously maintained, that even a 7-Eleven convenience store feels like a cultural experience. Matcha everything. Minimalist design that makes you question every cluttered room you've ever lived in. A level of craft and attention to detail that borders on spiritual.

What actually moved me

  • Fushimi Inari (Kyoto) — 10,000 torii gates climbing a mountain. Go at 5am to have it to yourself. At 10am it's a tourist traffic jam.
  • Shinjuku at night — neon, yakitori smoke, salary men, tourists all layered on top of each other. Overwhelming and magnificent.
  • A neighbourhood izakaya — no English menu, no tourists, just locals sharing food and sake. Point at things. You'll be fine.
  • Senso-ji Temple (Tokyo) — ancient Buddhist temple in the middle of a metropolis. The contrast never gets old.

Indian travellers will find unexpected parallels — the reverence for tradition, the multi-generational businesses, the pride in craft. Japan feels both completely foreign and somehow familiar in its values.

Food 10 / 10

Japan has more Michelin stars than any other country on earth. Tokyo alone has more than Paris. But the real story isn't the fancy restaurants — it's that average food here is extraordinary. A ₹550 ($7) bowl of ramen from a basement shop will be the best thing you eat all week.

What to eat

  • Ramen — each region has its own style. Tokyo (shoyu, clear), Hokkaido (miso, rich), Hakata (tonkotsu, creamy). Budget ₹550–900 (~$7–11 / ¥1,000–1,600) per bowl.
  • Conveyor belt sushi (kaiten-zushi) — 2 pieces per plate at ₹110–165 (~$1.30–2 / ¥200–300). A full meal for ₹800 ($10). Surreal quality.
  • Gyudon (beef rice bowl) — Yoshinoya or Sukiya, ₹330 (~$4 / ¥600). Japan's greatest fast food.
  • Takoyaki — octopus balls from an Osaka street stall. 8 pieces for ₹330 (~$4 / ¥600).
  • 7-Eleven onigiri — rice triangle for ₹110 (~$1.30 / ¥200). Don't judge it. Eat it.

For vegetarians: the honest truth

Japan is hard for vegetarians. Most broths — ramen, miso soup, even some sauces — contain dashi (fish/pork stock). The dish can look vegetarian but isn't. Buddhist shojin ryori restaurants are fully vegan and exceptional, but expensive (₹3,300+ / ~$40 / ¥6,000+ per person). Download HappyCow before you go. Tokyo and Osaka have good vegetarian options. Rural areas are difficult.

Safety 10 / 10

Japan is the safest country most travellers will ever visit. The statistics are surreal — Tokyo, a city of 14 million people, has a lower crime rate than most small European towns. Wallets left on tables at cafes get handed in to the police. Umbrellas left on trains are neatly stored at the station office.

Solo women travellers consistently rate Japan as among the safest destinations in the world. That said, dense crowds like Shibuya Crossing or weekend Harajuku can get chaotic — petty jostling happens. Keep your bag in front and you'll be fine.

Safety verdict

Walk anywhere, at any hour, alone or with family. Japan is genuinely safe in a way that will make you recalibrate what "normal" feels like back home.

Transport 9 / 10

Japan's public transport is the best in the world. Not a debate. Trains arrive to the second. The network covers every corner of every major city. The Shinkansen (bullet train) connects Tokyo to Kyoto in 2 hours 15 minutes at 320 km/h. The minus one point? It's genuinely complex and can overwhelm first-time visitors.

What you need to know

  • IC Card (Suica/Pasmo) — tap-in, tap-out card for all metro and local trains. Top up with ₹550–1,100 (~$7–13 / ¥1,000–2,000) and cover a full day. Essential — get one at the airport on arrival.
  • JR Pass — unlimited Shinkansen + JR trains. 7-day pass costs ₹27,500 (~$330 / ¥50,000). Worth it if you're doing Tokyo + Kyoto + Osaka. Not worth it for Tokyo-only.
  • Google Maps in Japan — works perfectly for train navigation. Follow it blindly. It knows the transfers.
  • Taxis — expensive. ₹1,100 (~$13 / ¥2,000) gets you maybe 2km. Use trains.

The minus one for complexity: first-time visitors routinely board wrong train lines, take express when they needed local, or miss the platform in huge stations like Shinjuku (53 exits). Give yourself extra buffer time for the first two days.

Accommodation 8 / 10

Japan's accommodation options are unlike anywhere else in the world. From capsule hotels (a futuristic pod with your own locker, TV, and climate control) to tatami-mat ryokans with onsen baths and a kaiseki dinner included — the range is extraordinary.

Book accommodation 2–3 months ahead for March–May and October–November. Japan fills up fast in peak season. Business hotels are excellent value — tiny rooms but immaculately clean, with great shower pressure and usually a vending machine floor.

Value 6 / 10

Japan is expensive — let's not pretend otherwise. But the yen has weakened dramatically. In 2022, ₹100 got you ~160 yen. Today it gets you ~180 yen. Japan is effectively 12–15% cheaper than it was two years ago for Indian visitors. That shift is significant.

Things that are surprisingly cheap

  • Street food and convenience store meals (₹110–550 / ¥200–1,000)
  • Local metro (₹110–220 / ¥200–400 per ride)
  • Temple entry (often free or ₹220–550 / ¥400–1,000)
  • Most museums (₹550–1,100 / ¥1,000–2,000)

Things that will hurt your wallet

  • Ryokan with meals (₹11,000–33,000+ per night)
  • Taxis (absurdly expensive)
  • Themed experiences — teamLab, Universal Studios, Robot Restaurant
  • Alcohol — a beer at a Tokyo bar costs ₹550–1,100 (~$7–13 / ¥1,000–2,000)

Nightlife 7 / 10

Tokyo's nightlife is world-class but wallet-punishing. Shinjuku's Golden Gai — a maze of impossibly tiny bars seating 6–8 people each, each with its own music, personality, and sometimes a ¥500 cover charge — is one of the great bar experiences on earth. Shibuya has massive clubs. Roppongi caters to an international crowd (and is where most scams happen — avoid the touts).

Osaka's Dotonbori district is more casual and less expensive. Locals eat, drink, and be loud along the canal. More your speed if you want atmosphere without the Tokyo price tag.

A note: Japan doesn't really have a late-night food culture the way Southeast Asia does. Most restaurants close by 10–11pm. But convenience stores (7-Eleven, FamilyMart, Lawson) are open 24/7 and serve hot food at the counter. Japan's 7-Elevens are a different species from what you know.

Crowds 5 / 10

Overtourism is Japan's biggest problem right now. Kyoto in cherry blossom season is genuinely unpleasant — the famous bamboo grove at Arashiyama is wall-to-wall people taking the same photo. The iconic Fushimi Inari gates feel like a theme park queue at 11am.

This isn't Japan's fault. The weak yen has made it a bargain destination globally, and Chinese tourists returned post-COVID en masse. The crowds are real and will affect your experience if you go at the wrong time or the wrong hours.

How to escape the crowds

  • Go early — be at major sites by 7am. Before 8am, Fushimi Inari is genuinely peaceful.
  • Avoid March–May and October–November weekends — peak domestic tourism plus international peak.
  • Explore off-the-path — Kanazawa, Takayama, Nikko, Nara (weekday mornings). Fewer tourists, same magic.
  • Stay in a ryokan in a small town — rural Japan remains untouched and extraordinary.

What Nobody Tells You About Japan

  • Cash is still king — many restaurants, smaller shops, and rural areas are cash-only. Carry ¥10,000–20,000 in cash always. 7-Eleven ATMs accept international cards.
  • No rubbish bins anywhere — Japan is immaculate but there are almost no public bins. You carry your trash until you find one (usually at a convenience store).
  • Silence on public transport — talking loudly on the phone is genuinely rude. Trains are near-silent. The quiet is both jarring and wonderful.
  • Tattoos may bar entry to onsens — many traditional onsen (hot spring baths) prohibit tattoos. If you have visible tattoos, look for tattoo-friendly onsen specifically.
  • The Wi-Fi situation — rent a pocket Wi-Fi at the airport (₹550–830 / day, ~$7–10) or get a data SIM. Free Wi-Fi in Japan outside of tourist areas is surprisingly patchy.
  • Jet lag works in your favour — if you're coming from India (IST is 3.5 hours behind JST), you'll naturally wake at 5–6am. Perfect for beating the crowds at temples.

Final Verdict: 8.1 / 10

Japan is not just a travel destination — it's a reminder that a society can choose to operate differently. The cleanliness, the punctuality, the quiet pride in craft, the food at every price point — it's dizzying and beautiful. The crowds in peak season and the vegetarian challenge knock it down from a perfect score. But if you can time it right, go in shoulder season, and eat everything that isn't labeled vegan (and isn't) — Japan will ruin you for everywhere else. In the best possible way.
