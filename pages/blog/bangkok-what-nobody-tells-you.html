15 Things Nobody Tells You About Bangkok

You've read ten Bangkok blogs and they all say the same thing. Go to Wat Arun. Eat pad thai. Try a tuk-tuk. The photos are beautiful. The tips are useless. None of them tell you that the tuk-tuk driver is going to try to take you to a gem shop, or that fish sauce is in your "vegetarian" fried rice, or that the Grand Palace at 11am is just a slow-moving crowd of people who read the same blog as you. Check our Bangkok honest scorecard for the full picture — but these 15 things are what we wish someone had told us before we landed.

01
💰 Saves Money ⚠️ Avoid Mistake
Tuk-tuks are not how Bangkok people get around — they're a commission racket with an engine

Nobody in Bangkok takes a tuk-tuk to go somewhere. They're loud, they're hot, they get stuck in traffic like everything else, and they charge 3–5× what a Grab would cost for the same distance. The "iconic Bangkok tuk-tuk experience" that every blog romanticises is mostly just a way to be uncomfortable while spending more money.

Worse: a lot of tuk-tuk drivers work on commission. They'll tell you the temple you're heading to is "closed for a Buddhist ceremony today" (it isn't — this is a very old script, still running) and offer to take you somewhere else, which involves passing through a tailor or gem shop where they get a cut of whatever you spend.

Take one tuk-tuk ride if you want the memory of it. Negotiate the price first, keep it under 2km, and if the driver suddenly mentions a great shop or a temple that's "only open today" — get out. For actual getting around, Grab is always the answer.
02
⚠️ Avoid Mistake
You will be turned away at temple gates in shorts — and the sarong stall outside knows it

Bangkok in October feels like a furnace. You pack light. You show up to Wat Phra Kaew in shorts and a sleeveless top because it's 37 degrees and you're in a holiday mindset. The guard at the gate turns you away. Right outside, there's a stall renting sarongs for THB 100 (~₹230). This is not a coincidence.

Wat Phra Kaew, Wat Pho, and Wat Arun all strictly enforce the no-shorts, no-sleeveless rule. Indian travellers get caught by this more than most — back home, nobody stops you at a temple for wearing what you want. Bangkok temples are different.

Pack one pair of lightweight long trousers and keep a thin scarf in your bag for shoulder cover. Takes up no space, saves you ₹200 and the awkwardness of negotiating sarong rentals in 38-degree heat.
03
💰 Saves Money
The airport taxi drivers quote flat rates to tourists — the meter tells a very different story

You land at Suvarnabhumi, you're tired, and a driver at the taxi rank says "800 baht, Sukhumvit." That sounds reasonable until you learn the meter fare for the same journey is THB 230 plus about THB 75 in expressway tolls. You just paid double. This happens to almost every first-timer.

Book Grab before you leave the arrivals hall — it'll show you the exact price upfront (THB 200–320 to the city depending on your hotel). The Grab pickup zone at Suvarnabhumi is in the public transport area, not the main taxi rank. Follow signs for "Taxi Meter / Public Transport" and look for the rideshare section.

Even faster: the Airport Rail Link from Suvarnabhumi to Phaya Thai station takes 28 minutes and costs THB 45 (~₹100). If your hotel is anywhere near the BTS Skytrain network, this beats everything — no traffic, no price negotiation, just a train.
04
⚠️ Avoid Mistake
The BTS and MRT look like one metro system on Google Maps — they're not, and you pay twice when you switch

Bangkok has two separate rail networks run by two different companies with two completely different ticketing systems. The BTS Skytrain is elevated and covers Sukhumvit, Silom, and the main tourist corridor. The MRT is underground and covers Chinatown, the Old Town area, and different parts of the city. Google Maps uses both and routes you through them as if it's one network. It's not.

When you transfer from BTS to MRT at an interchange station, you exit one system and pay to enter the other. The BTS day pass you bought is worthless on the MRT platform. This catches a surprising number of travellers on their first day.

Buy single-journey tokens for whichever network you're on — it's cheaper than a day pass unless you're doing 5+ trips on the same line. For anything the metro doesn't reach, Grab costs almost nothing within the city. THB 50–80 (~₹120–190) gets you most places.

05
⚠️ Avoid Mistake 🏛️ Culture
Your "vegetable fried rice" has fish sauce in it — Thai food is not vegetarian-friendly by default

Fish sauce is the invisible ingredient in Thai cooking. It goes into pad thai, fried rice, curries, stir-fries, dipping sauces, and the cooking liquid for most dishes. When you order "vegetable fried rice," the vegetables are real but the fish sauce is also real. The chef isn't trying to trick you — it's just how the dish is made.

The word that changes everything: Jay (เจ, pronounced like "jay"). It's Thai Buddhist vegan — no meat, no fish sauce, no oyster sauce, no eggs. Look for yellow flags with เจ written on them outside restaurants. In Chinatown especially, these are everywhere during Buddhist holiday periods. Two more useful phrases: mai sai nam pla (no fish sauce) and mai sai nuea sat (no meat).

Where to eat reliably as a vegetarian: Jay restaurants on Yaowarat Road (Chinatown) · May Kaidee near Khao San Road (veg-only, been around forever) · Indian restaurants near Silom MRT · Fresh fruit carts everywhere — mango, pineapple, watermelon cut to order for THB 20–40
06
⚠️ Avoid Mistake
The gem scam near Bangkok temples has been running since the 1980s — it's still catching people today

Near every major Bangkok temple, there's a well-dressed local who will strike up a conversation, ask where you're from, be delighted you're Indian, mention something about a government gem sale or special export event happening only today, and offer to take you there. The gems are real. They're also priced at 10× their actual value and can't be resold anywhere. People lose lakhs to this.

The tuk-tuk variant is the same playbook: driver mentions your temple is closed for a ceremony, offers a detour, the detour passes a tailor or jewellery shop. The driver gets THB 200–500 commission per tourist who enters.

Nobody approaches strangers near temples out of friendliness. It doesn't happen. If someone starts a conversation near Wat Phra Kaew or the Grand Palace and it leads anywhere other than "enjoy your visit," walk away. You are not being rude. You are being sensible.
07
💰 Saves Money 🌟 Unlock Experience
There's a ₹35 river ferry that passes Wat Arun, Wat Pho, and the Grand Palace — tourists pay 10× for the same view

The Chao Phraya Express Boat is Bangkok's working river commuter ferry — used every morning by residents going to work. It costs THB 15 (~₹35) per boarding and runs past Wat Arun, Wat Pho, the Grand Palace waterfront, old Chinese merchant buildings, and covered markets you'll never find on a tourist bus. Bangkok from the water is a completely different city.

Tourist boat companies do the exact same route for THB 200–400 per person, with a guide who reads descriptions off a laminated card. The express boat has no guide. It also has no tourists. That's the entire advantage.

Board at Sathorn Pier (Central Pier) — one stop from Saphan Taksin BTS. Take the orange-flag boat heading north. Get off at Tha Chang pier for the Grand Palace or Tha Tien pier for Wat Pho. Boats run every 15–20 minutes, 6am to 7pm. THB 15 each way.
08
💰 Saves Money
The Grand Palace at 11am is just 8,000 tourists shuffling forward — go at 8:30 or don't bother

Wat Phra Kaew and the Grand Palace complex gets 8 million visitors a year. The tour buses arrive between 9:30 and 10am. By 11am the courtyard is genuinely wall-to-wall — you're shuffling, not walking, and you can't see the buildings properly because there are so many people between you and them. Travellers who visit at this time wonder what everyone is raving about.

Gates open at 8:30am. Arrive then. The first 45 minutes you'll have the courtyards to yourself in relative terms — you can stop, look up, actually take in what you're standing in front of. It's the same place, completely different experience. Late afternoon (3:30–5pm closing time) is the other good window — crowds thin and the light turns golden. Entry is THB 500 (~₹1,100).

Wat Pho (the Reclining Buddha) is literally across the road — THB 200 entry. Do the Grand Palace first, then Wat Pho. These two are a half-day. Don't add Wat Arun the same morning — you'll rush everything and enjoy nothing.
09
💰 Saves Money 🌟 Unlock Experience
Bangkok's rooftop bars look free on Instagram — there's a minimum spend you only find out when you're already up there

Sky Bar at Lebua (the Hangover bar), Octave Rooftop, Three Sixty Jazz Lounge — these places have minimum spend policies that work like a cover charge. One cocktail runs THB 400–700 (~₹950–1,650). Most require two drinks per person minimum. You're looking at ₹2,000–3,500 per person before you've ordered any food. The views are real and spectacular. Just know the cost before you go up.

If you want the Bangkok skyline without the surprise bill: King Power Mahanakhon SkyWalk is the observation deck of Bangkok's tallest building — THB 880 (~₹2,000) per person, fixed price, glass floor, best 360° view in the city. You pay at the entrance, no minimum spends, no pressure.

Icon Siam mall's riverside terrace and Terminal 21's rooftop food court have genuinely good views at food-court prices. Not the same drama as Sky Bar, but you'll enjoy the meal more when you're not calculating your minimum spend mid-sentence.
10
🌟 Unlock Experience 💰 Saves Money
Chatuchak at 9am and Chatuchak at noon are two completely different places — one is exciting, one is a punishment

15,000 stalls, 200,000 people on a busy Saturday. By 11am the good vintage pieces are gone, the heat is unbearable, the aisles are impossible to move through, and you'll be sweating so much you won't want to try on anything anyway. People who arrive at noon and wander around for an hour wondering what the fuss is about — this is why.

Get there at 9am when it opens. The stalls are fresh, the temperature is manageable, and you can actually stop and look at things. Section 2–4 for vintage clothing, Section 8 for Thai crafts and ceramics, Section 22 for antiques and old things. Download the Chatuchak Market app — it's rough but it maps out which section has what.

Saturday and Sunday only, 9am–6pm. MRT to Chatuchak Park or BTS to Mo Chit. Wear the absolute lightest clothes you own. There is almost no shade inside and Bangkok at 10:30am is not something you want to experience in jeans.
11
🏛️ Culture 🌟 Unlock Experience
National Museum Bangkok is free on Sundays and almost nobody goes — it's one of the best museums in Southeast Asia

The National Museum Bangkok sits right next to the Grand Palace and almost everyone walks past it. Free on Sundays (regular entry THB 200 on other days). It's an 1874 palace complex with Thai art, Buddhist sculpture, royal regalia, weapons, ceramics, and historical objects spanning a thousand years. Sunday morning it's quiet. It should not be this easy.

The Jim Thompson House nearby is different — not a museum exactly, but a beautifully restored traditional Thai house that belonged to an American silk merchant who disappeared mysteriously in 1967. Still unexplained. The house itself is worth the THB 200 entry — guided tours only, runs at fixed times, book when you arrive.

Indian travellers specifically: the National Museum has a section on Indian influence in Thai culture — the Sanskrit origins of the Thai script, Hindu cosmology in royal iconography, Buddhist art that came through India. Walking through it, a lot feels familiar in a way you won't expect.

12
🌟 Unlock Experience
Monsoon Bangkok isn't constant rain — it's one heavy downpour in the afternoon and then it's over

June to October is monsoon season and most people avoid Bangkok during this period, imagining days of grey drizzle. What actually happens: the morning is clear, you do your temples, you have lunch, it absolutely pours for about 90 minutes sometime between 2pm and 5pm, and then it stops and the evening is fine. This pattern holds for most of the monsoon season most of the time.

The advantages: flights are 30–40% cheaper than peak season. Hotels negotiate. Chatuchak is less packed. The temples have shorter queues. The actual downside is flooding — Chinatown and some low-lying areas can get ankle-deep water during very heavy rain. Stay near BTS stations and you'll be fine.

Start outdoor things by 8am, be inside a restaurant or mall by 3pm, resume after 6pm. That's the monsoon Bangkok schedule. Once you're expecting the afternoon rain, it stops feeling like a problem and starts feeling like a built-in break.
13
💰 Saves Money
Bangkok's 7-Elevens are not what you're imagining — Bangkok residents eat from them regularly

Thai 7-Elevens sell hot steamed buns (salapao) cooked fresh behind the counter, cup noodles with boiling water on tap, pre-packed rice meals, fresh fruit cups, yoghurt, boiled eggs, and decent sandwiches. Thais eat from these stores daily — it's not a desperate tourist backup, it's just convenient food. At 1am when nothing else is open and you're hungry, this fact is extremely useful.

THB 25–60 per item (~₹60–140). There's one every 200 metres in the tourist areas, open 24 hours. For vegetarians specifically — when you're stuck in a neighbourhood with no Jay restaurants and the street food is all meat — 7-Eleven is often the most reliable option in the city.

Go for: steamed buns (look for egg-filled ones for veg), fresh pineapple or mango cups, cold Thai milk tea pouches, green tea. The warm food section near the register changes throughout the day — early morning has the best selection.
14
🌟 Unlock Experience
Bangkok + Chiang Mai is a 1-hour flight and makes for a far better Thailand trip than 7 days in Bangkok alone

Bangkok runs out of genuinely new things to do around day 5. Most Indian travellers who stay 7 days find themselves back in the same malls, the same Sukhumvit streets, the same night markets. The fix is simple: 4 days Bangkok, then fly to Chiang Mai. One hour on Thai Lion Air or Nok Air, THB 500–1,200 one-way (~₹1,100–2,700). You land in a completely different country within the same country.

Chiang Mai has cooler mountain air, a walkable old city inside an ancient moat, ethical elephant sanctuaries, night bazaars that are less chaotic than Bangkok's, and food that's genuinely cheaper. The overnight train from Bangkok (THB 300–1,200 for a sleeper berth, 12 hours) is an experience in itself if you have the time.

Vegetarians: Chiang Mai is a relief after Bangkok. The Buddhist influence is stronger, there are Jay restaurants at every corner near the old city, and the Sunday night market on Wualai Road has dedicated vegetarian food stalls. Worth the flight just for the food.
15
💰 Saves Money 🌟 Unlock Experience
4–5 days in Bangkok is enough — after that you're just refilling your water bottle in the same air-conditioned malls

Bangkok is genuinely great. For about four days. The temples, Chatuchak, the river, Chinatown, the rooftop, the street food — you can do all of it well, without rushing, in a 4–5 day trip. Day 5 you start to repeat yourself. Day 6 you're walking through a mall because it's air-conditioned. Day 7 you're booking a massage because you've run out of things on your list.

If you have a week or more for Thailand, don't spend it all in Bangkok. Three nights Bangkok, then Chiang Mai or a beach (Koh Samui, Koh Lanta) — that trip is dramatically better than seven nights in Bangkok alone. The city is designed for intensity, not length.
